Should I Operate as a Sole Trader or a Company?

Choosing the right business structure is an important decision. It can affect how your business operates, how you pay tax, your personal risk, ongoing administration and your plans for future growth.

New Zealand Business Guide Business Structure

Why does your business structure matter?

When starting or growing a business in New Zealand, one of the first decisions you may need to make is how the business should be structured. You might operate as a sole trader, establish a limited liability company or work with others through a partnership.

Your business structure determines how the business is owned, how profits are treated, who is responsible for its obligations and what legal and administrative requirements apply. It can also influence how easily you bring in another owner, employ people or prepare the business for future changes.

There is no single structure that is best for every business. A self-employed consultant may have very different needs from a business with several owners, employees, significant equipment or plans to attract investors.

A decision worth reviewing

Your business structure should support both your current operations and your longer-term plans. It is worth reviewing your options before registering a business or making a significant change.

What is a sole trader?

A sole trader operates a business personally rather than through a separate company. The individual owns and runs the business and is generally entitled to its profits after business expenses have been paid.

This is a common structure for contractors, consultants, tradespeople, freelancers and individuals starting a small business. It is often attractive because it can be relatively straightforward to establish and manage.

A sole trader generally includes business income in their individual income tax arrangements. They are also responsible for managing their own business records, expenses, tax payments and any applicable GST or employment obligations.

Potential advantages of a sole trader structure

  • Simpler setup: Generally involves fewer formal establishment requirements than setting up a company.
  • Direct control: You make the day-to-day business decisions and manage operations yourself.
  • Less formal administration: There are generally fewer company-specific filing and governance requirements.
  • Straightforward ownership: You own the business directly and do not need to manage a shareholder structure.
  • Suitable for many small operations: Can be a practical starting point for independent contractors and smaller service businesses.

Things to consider

  • You are generally personally responsible for business debts and obligations.
  • Your personal assets may be exposed if the business cannot meet its obligations.
  • Bringing in investors or transferring ownership may be less straightforward.
  • You need to manage business and personal financial responsibilities carefully.

What is a limited liability company?

A limited liability company is a separate legal entity from the people who own it. The company can enter into contracts, own assets, borrow money and carry on business in its own name.

The people who own a company are known as shareholders. Companies also have directors who are responsible for managing the company and meeting their legal duties.

A company structure may suit businesses that plan to expand, employ staff, introduce additional owners or establish a more formal ownership arrangement.

A company can provide a separation between the company's obligations and the personal obligations of its shareholders. However, limited liability is not absolute. Personal guarantees, director duties, misconduct and other circumstances can still create personal exposure.

Potential advantages of a company

  • Separate legal identity: The company operates as a legal entity separate from its shareholders.
  • Defined ownership: Shares can provide a clear way to record ownership interests.
  • Growth opportunities: A company structure may make it easier to introduce additional shareholders or investors.
  • Continuity: The company can continue operating when individual shareholders change.
  • Formal structure: Can provide a more structured framework for ownership, management and business planning.

Things to consider

  • There are company registration and ongoing administrative responsibilities.
  • Companies must meet applicable legal, accounting and reporting requirements.
  • Directors have legal duties and responsibilities that must be understood.
  • Accounting, compliance and administration costs may be higher than for a sole trader.

What about a partnership?

A partnership is generally formed when two or more people or organisations operate a business together and share its profits and responsibilities.

Partnerships can be useful when business owners bring different skills, experience, professional qualifications or financial resources to the same business. For example, two professionals may decide to combine their expertise and operate a shared practice.

A partnership can have different tax and legal responsibilities from a sole trader or company. In general, the partnership's income is allocated to the partners according to the relevant arrangements, and partners account for their respective shares.

Partners may also have personal responsibility for partnership obligations. It is important to understand how responsibility is shared before entering into a partnership.

Important points to agree on

  • How profits and losses will be shared.
  • Each partner's responsibilities and contributions.
  • How important business decisions will be made.
  • How disagreements will be handled.
  • What happens if a partner retires, leaves or wants to sell their interest.

A written partnership agreement can help clarify expectations and reduce uncertainty. Professional legal and accounting advice is particularly useful when setting up a partnership.

Sole trader vs company vs partnership: Key differences

The following table provides a general overview of how the three common structures compare. The actual legal and tax position will depend on your circumstances and the applicable rules.

Consideration Sole Trader Company Partnership
Legal identity The individual operates the business. Separate legal entity. Partners operate the business together.
Ownership Owned by one individual. Owned by shareholders. Owned by two or more partners.
Setup Generally simpler. Requires company registration. Requires agreement between partners and appropriate setup.
Administration Generally fewer formal requirements. More formal company administration and filing requirements. Depends on the partnership and its arrangements.
Tax treatment Business profit is generally included in the individual's taxable income. The company generally files its own income tax return. Partnership income is generally allocated to individual partners.
Liability Individual is generally personally responsible for business obligations. Shareholder liability is generally limited, subject to exceptions. Partners may have personal responsibility for partnership obligations.
Decision-making Controlled by the individual. Managed by directors, with shareholder rights. Shared according to the partnership arrangements.
Growth and ownership Bringing in investors may be less straightforward. Shares can provide a structure for multiple owners and investors. Additional partners may be possible under agreed arrangements.

This comparison is a general guide rather than legal or tax advice. Specific obligations and outcomes depend on the structure, business activities and individual circumstances.

Does operating through a company automatically reduce tax?

Not necessarily. Choosing a company structure does not automatically mean you will pay less tax overall. The tax outcome depends on several factors, including business profits, how money is taken out of the company, shareholder circumstances and the applicable tax rules.

A company generally pays income tax on its taxable profit. If profits are later distributed to shareholders, there may be additional tax considerations. The treatment depends on how the funds are distributed and the circumstances involved.

A sole trader, on the other hand, generally pays income tax on business profits through their individual tax arrangements. The applicable individual tax rates and other circumstances need to be considered.

Look beyond the headline tax rate

A useful comparison should consider the total tax position, how you intend to use business profits, administration costs, compliance responsibilities and your future plans. The lowest apparent tax rate does not always mean the most suitable structure.

How does your business st]ructure affect finances and record keeping?

Regardless of which structure you choose, maintaining accurate financial records is essential. You need to understand your income, expenses, cash flow, tax obligations and overall financial performance.

The way you manage business money may differ depending on your structure. A company should maintain clear records of its own transactions and distinguish company funds from personal funds. Sole traders should also keep reliable business records and clearly identify business-related transactions.

Good bookkeeping helps you understand whether the business is profitable, identify expenses, prepare financial statements and meet tax reporting responsibilities.

Financial practices worth establishing early

Separate finances

Keep business transactions organised and distinguish business funds from personal spending.

Keep supporting records

Retain invoices, receipts, bank records and other documents relevant to your business transactions.

Monitor performance

Review income, expenses, profit and cash flow regularly to understand how the business is performing.

Plan for tax

Set aside funds and plan for relevant tax payments and filing deadlines.

What should you consider before choosing?

Before deciding on a business structure, consider how you intend to operate, the risks involved and what you want the business to achieve. The right choice should make sense financially and practically.

1. Number of owners

Are you starting alone, working with a business partner or planning to bring in additional shareholders in the future?

2. Business risk and liability

Consider the type of work you perform, the contracts you enter into, potential debts and the level of personal exposure involved.

3. Expected income and profitability

Consider your expected revenue, expenses, business profits and how you intend to use the money generated by the business.

4. Employees and contractors

Think about whether you will employ staff, engage contractors or need additional systems to manage payroll and other responsibilities.

5. Setup and ongoing costs

Compare registration, accounting, compliance and administration costs before making your decision.

6. Future growth plans

Consider whether you plan to expand, introduce investors, open additional locations or eventually sell the business.

Which structure might suit different businesses?

Looking at practical examples can help explain why different structures may suit different business situations. These examples are illustrative only and are not recommendations for a particular business.

Independent consultant

Someone providing professional services independently with limited operating costs and no co-owners.

Possible option: A sole trader structure may be a straightforward starting point.

Growing retail business

A business planning to employ staff, expand operations and potentially introduce additional owners.

Possible option: A company structure may provide a suitable ownership framework.

Two business professionals

Two people combining their experience and resources to provide services together.

Possible option: A partnership or company may be considered depending on their ownership and risk requirements.

Business seeking investment

A business intending to raise capital or provide ownership interests to additional investors.

Possible option: A company may provide a more suitable shareholding structure.

Can you change your business structure later?

Yes, it may be possible to move from operating as a sole trader to running a company as your business develops. A business may also change its ownership arrangements or move from a partnership to another structure.

However, changing a business structure is not always a simple administrative task. Depending on the circumstances, it may involve transferring assets, reviewing contracts, changing registrations, updating insurance arrangements and considering the tax implications.

You may also need to review existing debts, finance arrangements, employees, GST registration and other obligations. The impact will depend on the nature of the business and the proposed change.

Plan a change before making it

Before changing your structure, discuss the legal, accounting and tax implications with the appropriate professionals. Planning ahead can help avoid unnecessary costs and unexpected complications.

When should you review your business structure?

Your original business structure may continue to work well for years. However, as your circumstances change, it is sensible to check whether it still meets your needs.

You may want to review your structure when:

  • Your business income or profitability changes significantly.
  • You are planning to bring in a business partner or investor.
  • You intend to employ staff or expand your operations.
  • Your business activities or risk exposure change.
  • You are considering selling or transferring the business.
  • Your personal, financial or long-term business goals change.

Frequently asked questions

Here are answers to some common questions about choosing and managing a business structure in New Zealand.

Is it better to be a sole trader or a limited company?
There is no universally better option. A sole trader structure may suit someone who wants to operate independently with simpler administration. A company may be more appropriate for a business that needs a separate legal entity, multiple shareholders or a more formal ownership structure. The best option depends on your business activities, financial position, risk and future plans.
Do I need to register a company to start a business in New Zealand?
No. You can generally start operating as a sole trader without incorporating a company. However, you still need to meet the relevant tax, registration and business obligations for your activities. You should check which registrations apply to your particular situation.
Does a company protect my personal assets?
A company is a separate legal entity, and shareholder liability is generally limited. However, protection is not absolute. Personal guarantees, director duties, personal misconduct and other circumstances may create personal liability. You should obtain appropriate legal advice about your particular risks.
Will I pay less tax if I set up a company?
Not automatically. Your overall tax position depends on business profits, how money is distributed, your personal circumstances and the applicable tax rules. Company administration and compliance costs should also be considered when comparing structures.
Can I change from a sole trader to a company?
Yes, it may be possible to establish a company and transfer business activities to it. However, you should consider the tax, legal, accounting, asset transfer, contract and registration implications before making the change.
Do I need an accountant if I am a sole trader?
You are not necessarily required to hire an accountant, but professional accounting support can help you understand your tax responsibilities, maintain accurate records, manage expenses and make informed financial decisions. The level of support you need will depend on your business.
Can two people run a business as sole traders together?
Two people can each operate their own sole trader businesses and work together under suitable arrangements. However, if they jointly own and operate a business and share profits, a partnership or company structure may need to be considered. The actual legal relationship depends on the arrangements and activities involved.
Where can I find official information about business structures in New Zealand?
Business.govt.nz provides guidance on choosing a business structure. The Companies Office provides information about company registration and responsibilities, while Inland Revenue provides information about tax obligations. You can also speak with an accountant or lawyer about your circumstances.
